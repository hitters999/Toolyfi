What Is a Unit Converter?

A unit converter is a tool that converts a quantity expressed in one unit of measurement into an equivalent quantity in a different unit. For example, converting 5 kilometers into miles, or 100 grams into ounces, or 37°C into Fahrenheit.

The need for unit conversion arises because different countries, industries, and historical traditions use different systems of measurement. The two most common systems in use today are the International System of Units (SI), also known as the metric system, and the US Customary / Imperial system used predominantly in the United States and, historically, in the United Kingdom.

In Pakistan, a third layer exists: traditional South Asian units like tola (used for gold), marla and kanal (used for land), and seer and maund (used for agricultural goods and trade). These units remain in everyday use and are not covered by most international unit converter websites.

Weight Conversion — Including Pakistani Units

Weight (technically mass) conversion is used everywhere from cooking to trade to medical dosing. The SI base unit is the kilogram, while the US uses pounds and ounces. In Pakistan, traditional units remain in active use:

  • Tola — 1 tola = 11.6638 grams. Primarily used for gold and precious metals. When you see gold prices per tola in Pakistan, this is the unit being referenced.
  • Seer — 1 seer = 933.1 grams (~0.933 kg). Used in traditional markets and agriculture.
  • Maund — 1 maund = 37.324 kilograms (40 seers). Used for agricultural produce, cotton, and wholesale trade.

💡 Gold buyers note: Gold in Pakistan is priced per tola. 1 tola = 11.6638 grams. So if you want to convert a 10-gram gold piece to tola: 10 ÷ 11.6638 = 0.857 tola. Use our Weight converter above with the Tola option.

Temperature Conversion — Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin

Temperature is one of the most searched unit conversions globally, especially for weather, cooking, and science. The three main temperature scales are:

  • Celsius (°C) — Used in most of the world including Pakistan. Water freezes at 0°C, boils at 100°C.
  • Fahrenheit (°F) — Used primarily in the USA. Water freezes at 32°F, boils at 212°F.
  • Kelvin (K) — The SI unit for thermodynamic temperature. Used in physics and chemistry. 0 K = absolute zero = -273.15°C.

The key formulas are: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32 | °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 | K = °C + 273.15

Data Storage Conversion

In the digital age, data storage unit conversion is increasingly important. Understanding the difference between MB and GB — and why your 128 GB phone seems to only hold 119 GB of usable space — requires knowing how these units work:

  • 1 Kilobyte (KB) = 1,024 Bytes
  • 1 Megabyte (MB) = 1,024 KB = 1,048,576 Bytes
  • 1 Gigabyte (GB) = 1,024 MB
  • 1 Terabyte (TB) = 1,024 GB

💡 Why does your 128GB phone show less? Manufacturers use 1 GB = 1,000 MB (decimal), while operating systems use 1 GB = 1,024 MB (binary). This 2.4% difference explains why a "128 GB" phone shows ~119 GB in settings.

What a Unit Conversion Actually Does

A unit conversion expresses the same quantity in a different measurement unit. It does not change the object or amount itself. A length of one metre can be written as 100 centimetres, 1,000 millimetres, or about 39.37 inches. The quantity is the same; the chosen unit changes how the quantity is communicated. A useful converter makes that relationship visible, keeps the source and destination units explicit, and gives the user a result that can be checked before it is copied elsewhere.

This distinction matters because words such as weight, mass, volume, capacity, and size are often used loosely in ordinary conversation. The page groups comparable measurements together, but it does not turn one type of measurement into another when a material property is required. For example, litres cannot be converted directly to kilograms without knowing the density of the material. A cup of water and a cup of flour occupy a volume, but they do not have the same mass. Treat a result as valid only when the source and destination describe the same physical quantity.

Length, Distance, and Everyday Measurement

Length conversion is useful for dimensions, travel, clothing, construction, maps, and product specifications. The metric system commonly uses millimetres, centimetres, metres, and kilometres. US customary and imperial-style contexts often use inches, feet, yards, and miles. The exact relationships used here are fixed: an inch is exactly 2.54 centimetres, a foot is exactly 0.3048 metres, and a mile is exactly 1,609.344 metres. These fixed definitions make length conversion a good browser-side task.

Before calculating, identify whether the value refers to a line, a surface, or a volume. A room might be measured in metres for length, square metres for floor area, and cubic metres for capacity. Choosing the wrong category can produce a number that looks precise but describes the wrong thing. When recording a value, keep its unit beside it: write “2.4 m” rather than only “2.4.” The unit is part of the meaning.

Area, Marla, Kanal, and Property Context

Area measures a surface. Square metres, square feet, acres, and hectares are widely used, while marla and kanal are common in regional property conversations. On this page, the Area menu uses 25.2929 square metres for one marla and 505.857 square metres for one kanal. That corresponds to a 20-marla kanal in the calculation. Property terminology and locally recognized plot measures can differ across housing societies, cities, records, and contracts. For a purchase, sale, valuation, boundary, or title decision, rely on the measurement specified by the relevant legal or official documents rather than a general online result.

Use the converter to understand a stated size in a familiar unit, then verify the original schedule, map, or registry. Do not infer a property’s value, permitted use, construction area, or boundary from an area conversion alone. A square-foot figure can mean plot area, covered area, super area, or another defined term; the document context must decide which one applies.

Volume and Cooking Measurements

The Volume category converts listed capacity units such as litres, millilitres, gallons, cups, tablespoons, teaspoons, and cubic metres. It is especially helpful when a recipe, container, or technical specification uses unfamiliar units. Keep the qualifier where it matters: a US gallon and an imperial gallon are different amounts, and a cup can be defined differently by a recipe source. The menu labels distinguish the listed gallon options so you can choose deliberately.

A volume conversion does not create a reliable ingredient mass without a stated density. Recipes often give cups, while a kitchen scale may give grams; the relationship depends on the ingredient and how it is packed. Follow the source recipe or use a reliable ingredient-specific reference when precision matters. The converter remains useful for moving between volume units that describe the same capacity.

Data Storage: Binary and Decimal Labels

Data capacity is another place where the label matters. This page’s data category applies the binary factors defined in its conversion table: each larger step is 1,024 of the previous listed unit. Manufacturers and services can instead use decimal prefixes, where a kilobyte is 1,000 bytes. A device may therefore show a capacity differently from its marketing label without either number necessarily being an error. Read the product documentation and unit notation when comparing storage.

For file-transfer estimates, the displayed size is not the same as network speed. A megabyte measures stored data, while megabits per second measures a rate. Eight bits make one byte, so a value labelled Mbps cannot be compared directly with MB without converting the unit. Keeping the symbols visible helps avoid a common but costly misunderstanding.

Time, Speed, Pressure, and Energy

Time conversions are useful for schedules and durations, but calendar months and years are not fixed-length intervals. The page uses average values for its listed month and year entries, which makes them useful for estimates rather than calendar deadlines. Use an actual date or calendar tool where start and end dates matter. Speed conversion changes units such as kilometres per hour, miles per hour, metres per second, and knots. It does not account for route, traffic, wind, or vehicle performance.

Pressure and energy conversions deserve the same care. A pressure value may need a gauge-versus-absolute context. An energy value may be confused with power, which is energy per unit of time. The converter can transform among the units it lists; it cannot decide whether a measurement is safe, correctly measured, or suitable for a design. In technical work, retain significant figures, source conditions, and the original unit alongside the converted value.

Precision, Rounding, and Significant Figures

A calculator can display many decimal places even when the original measurement was approximate. If a tape measure reads roughly 2 metres, reporting 6.56167979 feet suggests a precision that the source does not support. Match your final rounding to the precision of the input and the needs of the task. A homework problem may expect a stated number of significant figures; a purchase order may require a specified decimal format; an informal estimate may need only a sensible rounded value.

The result panel gives a copy-ready value, but it is still your responsibility to select an appropriate presentation. Preserve extra precision during intermediate work when needed, then round deliberately at the final step. If you are unsure whether an input is exact or measured, record the source and do not let decimal places alone imply certainty.

When a Conversion Cannot Answer the Real Question

Some problems look like conversions but are actually decisions that need additional facts. A shipping question may require package dimensions, chargeable weight rules, carrier limits, and destination policy. A recipe question may require ingredient density and serving size. An electrical question may need voltage, current, phase, and equipment specifications. A property question may depend on surveyed boundaries, local records, and what a stated area term covers. The converter can make one measurement easier to read, but it cannot supply the missing context.

For that reason, avoid copying a calculated number into a regulated, legal, medical, financial, or safety-critical document without the required verification. Keep a note of the source value and its original unit, state the conversion factor or formula when a reader may need to reproduce it, and round only at the appropriate step. Good measurement work is as much about traceability as it is about calculation.

Using Results in Documents and Spreadsheets

When you paste a result into a spreadsheet, report, invoice, or message, include the unit and enough decimals for the audience. A value such as “3.28” has no meaning without a unit. “3.28 ft” is clearer, and “approximately 3.28 ft from 1 m” gives a reviewer even more context. If the destination uses a required format, follow that format. A spreadsheet may need a raw number in one cell and a unit in another; a technical drawing may need a standard symbol; a public explanation may need a plain-language equivalent.

The copy control on this page moves the visible result to your clipboard. It does not record the calculation history or preserve your source value for you. For important work, retain the original source independently. If a team needs a repeatable process, document the category, source unit, destination unit, rounding rule, and any regional convention. That makes the next check faster and reduces the risk that a later reader assumes a different basis.

Limits of Fixed-Factor Conversion

Fixed-factor conversions work well for the listed standard units because their relationships are defined. They are not appropriate for fluctuating or contextual values. Currency rates change and need a time-stamped source. Cooking “cups” can differ by convention. Commodity measures can depend on quality or local trading practice. Calendar months do not all have the same number of days. The page shows an average month and year in its Time category for estimate purposes; use actual dates for deadlines, payroll, schedules, or age calculations.

Mach is also context-sensitive because the speed of sound varies with conditions. A simple reference factor can be useful for an approximate browser calculation, but it should not be used for flight planning or engineering decisions. The same mindset applies across the page: understand what the chosen factor represents, then decide whether it fits the real-world task.
