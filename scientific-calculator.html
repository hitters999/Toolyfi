Complete Scientific Functions Guide

The fx-82ES PLUS covers every mathematical operation needed for school and university. Here is a detailed breakdown of every major function category:

1. Trigonometric Functions — sin, cos, tan

Trigonometry is the foundation of physics, engineering, architecture, navigation, and computer graphics. These three functions describe relationships between angles and sides of triangles:

  • sin (Sine) — Opposite side divided by hypotenuse. Used in wave motion, oscillation physics, electrical AC circuits, signal processing, and sound analysis.
  • cos (Cosine) — Adjacent side divided by hypotenuse. Critical in vector decomposition, structural load analysis, GPS triangulation, and optics.
  • tan (Tangent) — Sine divided by cosine (opposite/adjacent). Used in slope calculations, angle of elevation/depression, optics, and surveying.

Example: Find sin(30°) — Ensure DEG mode is active, enter 30, press sin. Answer: 0.5

Example: Find cos(60°) — Enter 60, press cos. Answer: 0.5

Example: Find tan(45°) — Enter 45, press tan. Answer: 1.0

2. Inverse Trigonometric Functions — sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹

Given a ratio, find the angle. Access via SHIFT + sin/cos/tan:

  • sin⁻¹ (arcsin) — Finds angle whose sine equals input. Range: −90° to +90°. Used in determining launch angles in projectile motion.
  • cos⁻¹ (arccos) — Finds angle whose cosine equals input. Range: 0° to 180°. Used in dot product calculations between vectors.
  • tan⁻¹ (arctan) — Finds angle whose tangent equals input. Range: −90° to +90°. Used in calculating gradient angles and direction of resultant vectors.

Example: sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30°. Enter 0.5 → SHIFT → sin.

3. Hyperbolic Functions — sinh, cosh, tanh

Hyperbolic functions appear in advanced mathematics, physics of special relativity, and electrical engineering. They are defined using exponential functions:

  • sinh(x) = (eˣ − e⁻ˣ)/2 — Used in heat conduction equations, fluid dynamics, and quantum mechanics.
  • cosh(x) = (eˣ + e⁻ˣ)/2 — Describes the catenary curve — the natural shape of a hanging chain or cable. Used in bridge and power line engineering.
  • tanh(x) = sinh(x)/cosh(x) — Used in neural network activation functions, signal compression, and special relativity velocity addition.

On this calculator, press SHIFT then sin/cos/tan to access sinh/cosh/tanh (when HYP mode is indicated).

4. Logarithmic Functions — log, ln

Logarithms convert multiplication into addition and handle extremely large or small numbers elegantly:

  • log (common logarithm, base 10) — Used in: pH chemistry (pH = −log[H⁺]), decibel sound levels (dB = 10·log(P₁/P₂)), earthquake Richter scale, and information theory.
  • ln (natural logarithm, base e) — Fundamental in calculus (integral of 1/x), population growth modeling, radioactive decay, compound interest, and thermodynamics.
  • 10^x (SHIFT + log) — Antilogarithm. Reverses the log operation. Used to find the original value after logarithmic transformation.
  • e^x (SHIFT + ln) — Exponential function. Models continuous growth: bacteria, compound interest, radioactive decay, and electrical capacitor charging.

Example: log(1000) = 3 (because 10³ = 1000)

Example: ln(e) = 1 (because e¹ = e = 2.71828...)

Example: 10^2.5 = 316.23

6. Permutations (nPr) and Combinations (nCr)

These are the core tools of probability and combinatorics:

  • nPr (Permutation) — Number of ordered arrangements of r items from n. Formula: n!/(n−r)!
    Example: How many ways can 4 students from a class of 10 finish in 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th place? 10P4 = 5040
  • nCr (Combination) — Number of unordered selections of r items from n. Formula: n!/(r!(n−r)!)
    Example: How many ways can a committee of 3 be chosen from 10 people? 10C3 = 120

On the real CASIO: enter n → press nPr or nCr → enter r → press =. These functions are heavily tested in O-level, A-level, and SAT probability sections.

7. Factorial (n!)

Factorial multiplies a number by every positive integer below it: n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × ... × 2 × 1

  • 5! = 120
  • 10! = 3,628,800
  • 0! = 1 (by definition)

Factorials appear in permutations, combinations, Taylor series (sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − ...), Poisson distribution, and binomial expansion.

8. Memory Functions — STO, RCL, Ans

Memory is one of the most powerful but underused features:

  • Ans — Automatically stores the last result. Pressing Ans inserts it into the next calculation. Essential for multi-step problems where you need the previous answer.
  • STO (Store) — Saves current display value into memory. Use SHIFT + RCL to clear memory.
  • RCL (Recall) — Retrieves the stored memory value.

Pro tip: For multi-step equation solving, store intermediate results in memory rather than rounding and retyping — this eliminates accumulated rounding error.

9. Absolute Value (Abs)

|x| returns the positive magnitude of a number regardless of sign. Abs(−7) = 7. Abs(7) = 7. Used in distance calculations, error analysis, and signal processing where negative values represent direction, not magnitude.

10. ENG (Engineering Notation)

Engineering notation restricts exponents to multiples of 3 (matching SI unit prefixes):

  • 10⁶ = Mega (MHz, MW)
  • 10³ = Kilo (kHz, kW, km)
  • 10⁻³ = Milli (mA, mV)
  • 10⁻⁶ = Micro (μA, μF)

This is essential in electrical engineering and physics where values span many orders of magnitude.

11. S⬌D (Fraction/Decimal Toggle)

Converts calculation results between decimal and fraction representation. For example, 0.25 can be displayed as 1/4. This is useful when working with exact fractional answers in algebra and fraction arithmetic problems.

12. Brackets — ( )

Brackets control the order of operations. The calculator follows BODMAS/PEMDAS strictly:

  • Without brackets: 1+2×3 = 7 (multiplication first)
  • With brackets: (1+2)×3 = 9 (bracket first)

Always use brackets for complex expressions like sin(30+15) — type sin(45), not sin30+15.

This is the single most common source of wrong answers in trigonometry problems. Understanding when to use each is essential:
