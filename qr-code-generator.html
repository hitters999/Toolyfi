What Is a QR Code?

QR stands for Quick Response. A QR code is a two-dimensional barcode that can store significantly more information than a traditional linear barcode. It was invented in 1994 by Denso Wave, a Japanese automotive components manufacturer, originally to track vehicles during manufacturing. Today, QR codes are used for an enormous variety of purposes — website links, contact information, payment systems, WiFi sharing, and more.

When you scan a QR code with your smartphone camera, the phone reads the pattern and translates it into the encoded data — typically opening a URL, connecting to WiFi, or displaying contact information.

Types of QR Codes

URL QR Codes

The most common type — encodes a website URL. When scanned, the phone's browser opens the URL directly. Used for restaurant menus, product pages, social media profiles, and landing pages.

WiFi QR Codes

Encodes network credentials (SSID, password, security type) so guests can connect without typing. Particularly useful for hotels, cafes, offices, and homes. Supported natively on iOS and Android.

Email QR Codes

Opens the phone's email app with a pre-filled recipient address and optional subject line. Great for business cards, feedback forms, and contact information.

Phone QR Codes

Encodes a phone number. Scanning prompts the user to call the number. Ideal for business cards, advertisements, and customer support materials.

Text QR Codes

Displays plain text when scanned. Useful for brief messages, instructions, or any content that doesn't fit other categories.

QR Code Error Correction Explained

Error correction is one of the most important but least understood aspects of QR codes. It determines how much of the QR code can be damaged, covered, or obscured while still remaining scannable.

  • Level L (Low) — 7% of data can be restored. Produces smaller, simpler QR codes. Best for clean digital environments.
  • Level M (Medium) — 15% recovery. Good balance for most uses. The default in our generator.
  • Level Q (Quartile) — 25% recovery. Good for environments where codes may get dirty or damaged.
  • Level H (High) — 30% recovery. Allows you to overlay a logo over the center of the QR code while keeping it scannable.

💡 Want to add your logo to a QR code? Use High error correction, download the PNG, then overlay your logo in the center using any image editor like Canva or Photoshop.

Best Practices for QR Code Design

  • Minimum size — For print, ensure QR codes are at least 2cm × 2cm. Smaller codes are difficult to scan.
  • Contrast — High contrast between dark and light areas is essential. Avoid light-on-light or dark-on-dark combinations.
  • Quiet zone — Always maintain white space (quiet zone) around the QR code — at least 4 modules wide.
  • Test before printing — Always scan your QR code with multiple devices before mass printing.
  • Shorten URLs — Shorter URLs create less complex QR codes that scan faster and can be printed smaller.
