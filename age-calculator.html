Why Age Calculation Is Harder Than It Looks

At first glance, calculating age seems as simple as subtracting birth year from current year. But that approach gives you the wrong answer most of the time. There are several factors that make accurate age calculation more complex:

  • Months have different lengths — February has 28 or 29 days, while other months have 30 or 31. A naive subtraction ignores this entirely.
  • Leap years — Every four years (with some exceptions), there is an extra day in February. Over a lifetime, this adds up significantly. A person who is 30 years old has lived through roughly 7-8 leap years.
  • Birthday hasn't occurred yet this year — If today is March 15 and your birthday is June 20, you are technically still one year younger than the simple subtraction suggests.
  • Timezone differences — For very precise calculations, the timezone of birth and current timezone can create a one-day difference near midnight.

Age Calculation Around the World

Different cultures calculate and express age differently. In most Western countries, a person's age is the number of completed birthdays — so on the day before your 25th birthday, you are still 24. This is called the Western age system.

In some East Asian countries, particularly in traditional Korean and Chinese contexts, a different system exists. In the traditional Korean system (called "Korean age"), everyone is considered one year old at birth, and everyone ages one year on January 1st — not on their actual birthday. This means a Korean-age person can be one or two years older than their Western-age equivalent.

Japan historically used a similar system before adopting the Western standard officially in the 1950s.

Our tool calculates age using the standard international (Western) system — the number of completed years since birth. This is the system used in most official and legal contexts worldwide.
